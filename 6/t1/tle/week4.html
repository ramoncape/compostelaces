Choosing a Good Home for Fish: Site & Water Requirements

Before a single fingerling ever touches the water, a fish farmer must ask: is this the right place, and is this the right water? Today you'll find out what makes a natural fish pond succeed — or fail.

Learning objectives

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • Identify the basic requirements for natural fish raising (site, soil, water, sunlight).
  • Explain why each requirement matters for fish survival and growth.
  • Judge whether a given pond site is suitable and defend your reasoning.
Warm-Up

Think first: Imagine you are given a small piece of land beside a creek in your barangay and asked, "Can we raise bangus (milkfish) or tilapia here?" What is the very first thing you would check before saying yes? Keep your answer in mind — you'll revisit it at the end of the lesson.

Lesson

What does a fish pond really need?

Natural fish raising (palaisdaan) means growing fish mostly on the food that occurs naturally in the pond — plankton, algae, and small organisms — with the farmer managing the environment rather than feeding fish entirely by hand. For this system to work, the site itself has to cooperate.

1. Site selection. A good pond site sits on low, flat land that is not prone to flash floods, since a flood can wash fish away or bring in polluted runoff. It should be reasonably close to a dependable water source and to roads, so farmers can bring in fingerlings and later transport their harvest.

2. Soil type. The pond bottom and dikes need soil that holds water rather than letting it drain away. Clay loam soil is ideal because it compacts well and seals the pond, unlike sandy or gravelly soil, which lets water seep out.

3. Water supply. The water must be sufficient in volume and clean — free from industrial waste, strong chemicals, or heavy silt. Farmers check simple signs: normal color and smell, and, when possible, pH and dissolved oxygen levels, since fish breathe through the oxygen dissolved in water.

4. Sunlight. Sunlight is actually a quiet partner in natural fish raising — it drives photosynthesis in algae and plankton, which become the fish's natural food. But a pond that is scorched by sun all day, with no shade at all, can overheat and lose oxygen, stressing the fish. Balance matters more than maximum exposure.

From Empty Pond to Living Pond: Construction, Stocking & Feeding

A good site is only the start. Today you'll learn how a pond is shaped, how it's filled with the right fish, and how it's fed and fertilized so it keeps producing.

  • Predict what happens when stocking or feeding is done incorrectly.

Lesson

Building and filling the pond

Pond construction requires sturdy dikes (the earth walls that hold water in), a water inlet to let clean water enter, and a water outlet to drain or manage water level. Some farms also build a separate, shallower nursery pond for very young fish before moving them to a larger rearing pond to grow.

Stocking means introducing young fish, called fingerlings, into the pond at the right stocking density — the number of fish per square meter. Too few wastes space and natural food; too many causes crowding, competition for oxygen, and stunted growth.

Fertilization comes next. Farmers often apply organic fertilizer (like composted animal manure) or inorganic fertilizer to the pond before or during stocking. This isn't food for the fish directly — it nourishes the plankton and algae that the fish then eat, which is the heart of "natural" fish raising.

Feeding can be entirely natural (relying on plankton) or supplemented with extra feed, especially as fish grow larger and natural food alone isn't enough. Either way, farmers watch feeding behavior daily — fish that ignore food may signal poor water quality or illness.

Water management means keeping the water at a healthy depth, replacing stale water when needed, and watching for oxygen drops — often worst in the early morning, after algae have used up oxygen overnight instead of producing it.

Remember: fertilize the water, not the fish — the plankton do the rest.

Outside a Fish: External Anatomy

Every fin, scale, and gill cover on a fish has a job. Today you'll look closely at a fish's outside and connect its shape to how it swims, breathes, and senses the world.

Learning objectives

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • Identify the external parts of a fish and their functions.
  • Explain how each external part helps the fish survive in water.
  • Infer a fish's swimming behavior from its fin arrangement.
Warm-Up

Observe: Picture a fish swimming. Which part do you think does the most work to push it forward — and which parts do you think just keep it from tipping over?

Lesson

Parts of a fish, outside in

The mouth takes in food and its position (facing up, forward, or down) hints at what and where the fish eats. The eyes give vision without eyelids, since fish don't need to blink underwater.

Behind the eye sits the operculum, a hard gill cover that protects the delicate gills underneath and pumps water over them so the fish can breathe continuously, even while resting.

Fish have several kinds of fins, and each has a distinct role:

  • Dorsal fin (top) — keeps the fish upright and prevents rolling.
  • Pectoral fins (sides) — steer, brake, and help the fish move up or down.
  • Pelvic fins (belly) — add balance and stability.
  • Caudal fin (tail) — the main engine, sweeping side to side to push the fish forward.

Running along the fish's side is the lateral line, a row of sensory cells that detect vibrations and pressure changes in the water — essentially letting the fish "feel" movement from predators, prey, or other fish nearby, even in murky water.

Finally, scales cover the body, offering protection, while a thin layer of mucus reduces drag in the water and helps guard against infection.

Inside a Fish — and Bringing It All Together

What's happening beneath the scales keeps a fish alive: breathing, floating, digesting. Today you'll explore internal anatomy, then connect four days of learning into one big picture.

Learning objectives

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • Identify key internal organs of a fish and their functions.
  • Explain how water quality directly affects internal organ function.
  • Connect fish anatomy knowledge to real fish-farming decisions.
Warm-Up

Recall: On Day 1 you learned that fish need clean, oxygen-rich water. Which internal organ do you think depends on that oxygen the most? Hold that thought.

Lesson

What's working under the scales

Internally, the gills are made of thin filaments packed with blood vessels — this is where oxygen dissolved in water passes into the fish's blood, and carbon dioxide passes back out. It's the same job the operculum protects from the outside.

The heart, a simple two-chambered pump, moves blood first to the gills for oxygen, then out to the rest of the body.

The swim bladder (air bladder) is a gas-filled sac that lets a fish hold its position at a certain depth without constantly swimming — like an internal buoyancy control.

Food travels through the stomach and intestine for digestion, aided by the liver, while the kidney filters wastes and helps the fish balance salts and water in its body. A backbone (spinal cord) runs along the top, protecting the main nerve pathway and supporting the body's structure.

Here's the connection back to Day 1: if pond water is polluted or low in oxygen, it's the gills and heart that suffer first — which is exactly why site selection and water quality were the very first things a fish farmer had to get right.

Activity 5 · Diagnose the Pond (Reflection)

Scenario: A farmer's tilapia are gathering at the surface every morning, gulping air, and growth has slowed over the past two weeks. Using what you learned across all four days, write your diagnosis: what is likely wrong, and what should the farmer check or change?

Sample reasoning: Fish gasping at the surface usually points to low dissolved oxygen — often from overcrowding, too much organic waste, or an algae die-off overnight, which matches the Day 1–2 water and pond management lessons. Because oxygen enters through the gills (Day 4), stressed gills can also explain slowed growth, since the fish is spending energy just trying to breathe instead of growing. The farmer should test water quality, check stocking density, and consider adding water or aeration rather than simply feeding more.
Wrap-Up

Natural fish raising and fish anatomy are two sides of the same lesson: choosing the right site and water keeps the very organs you studied today healthy, and understanding those organs helps you understand why the site requirements matter in the first place.
